About me

I am a PhD student at the Graduate School of Public Policy, the University of Tokyo, advised by Professor Yoshito Takasaki. I study how environmental shocks such as rainfall and flooding affect violent conflict.

Research

Work in Progress

Water Troubles Downstream: How Rivers Carry Rainfall Shocks to Conflict in India

Previously circulated as “Dam It, Still No Peace? The Role of Dam Infrastructure in Mediating the Climate–Conflict Relationship in India”

Abstract: Most studies of rainfall and conflict assume that the only economically relevant rainfall is the one that fell locally or nearby. In a country crossed by large rivers that assumption may fail: rain that falls far upstream arrives downstream as river discharge and flooding. I study how upstream rainfall affects violent conflict downstream through river networks in India. I measure monsoon rainfall over each Indian district’s entire upstream drainage area on the hydrological network, and I simulate the river discharge this rain produces with two standard hydrological models. Upstream rainfall raises conflict events by 17 percent per standard deviation the following year, while the same monsoon’s local rain lowers them, so studies that use local rainfall alone mix two opposing effects. The simulated discharge intensifies conflict as well. The evidence points to the opportunity cost of fighting as the mechanism: the water arrives downstream as a hazard to farmers and lowers the return to legal work relative to fighting. The findings imply that flooding, not only drought, matters for conflict in India, and that existing policies and infrastructure may not be effective in mitigating a shock that arrives from upstream.
